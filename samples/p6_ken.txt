Interviewer: Tell me about your referral experience.

P6: Mixed, is the honest answer. The care I received was outstanding and the process of getting to it was appalling, and I have found it difficult to hold both of those in my head at once.

Interviewer: Start with the process.

P6: Referred in the March for the heart investigations. The first appointment letter came for a date I was told about four days beforehand, and I work offshore so I was in the North Sea. I rang to rearrange and was told that as I had declined an appointment I went back to the end of the list. Declined. I had not declined anything, I was on a rig. That word did something to me. It made it my fault.

Interviewer: How did you feel then?

P6: Furious, and then frightened, which is worse. Because underneath it I was worried about my heart and every week of the argument about dates was a week of not knowing. I stopped sleeping properly. My wife said I was snapping at everyone and she was right.

Interviewer: What happened next?

P6: My GP wrote a letter. Actually wrote a proper letter, not a form, explaining the offshore rotation. Three weeks later I had a date that worked. So a human being fixed it in a way the system could not. That is the pattern with all of this, if you ask me. Every good thing that happened to me happened because an individual person went outside the process.

Interviewer: And the care itself?

P6: Faultless. Genuinely. The cardiac team were brilliant, they explained everything, the nurse rang me two days later just to check I had understood. Nobody had to do that. I have thought since that if the front door worked half as well as the ward does, this would be the best service in the world.

Interviewer: Do you think the delay affected your outcome?

P6: They said not, and I believe them. But it affected me. There is a version of me that went into that four-month gap and a slightly different one that came out. I am more cynical now. I check things. I assume that if I do not follow up, nothing happens, and that has bled into other parts of my life, which my wife finds tiresome.

Interviewer: What would you say to somebody in the same position?

P6: Write everything down and be a nuisance. Which is a terrible thing to have to advise, because the people who most need to be seen are the least able to be a nuisance. The quiet ones and the polite ones lose. I was neither and I still nearly lost.

Interviewer: Any final thoughts?

P6: Only that I would not want the staff blamed for any of this. Every criticism I have is about arrangements, letters, systems, the way information does or does not move. Not one of them is about a person who treated me. That distinction matters to me and I do not think it always comes across when people complain.
