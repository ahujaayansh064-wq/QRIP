Interviewer: Thanks for making the time. Can you start by telling me what happened when you were first referred?

P1: My GP referred me in the January. She was lovely about it, she said it would be six weeks, maybe eight. I remember walking out of that appointment feeling like something was finally going to happen. I had been going back and forth for nearly a year by then with the stomach pain.

Interviewer: And what happened after the referral?

P1: Nothing. Absolutely nothing. No letter, no phone call, no text. I waited until March and then I started ringing. That was the beginning of the chasing, and honestly the chasing became a part-time job. You ring the hospital and they say ring the GP. You ring the GP and they say it is with the hospital now, we cannot see anything our end. Nobody could tell me where my referral actually was.

Interviewer: How did that feel?

P1: Infuriating. It made me feel invisible, like the letter had just fallen down the back of a filing cabinet somewhere and I was the only person in the world who cared. And then you start to doubt yourself. You think, maybe I am making a fuss, maybe the pain is not that bad, maybe they triaged me and decided I am not urgent and they just did not bother telling me. That is what got to me most, not being told.

Interviewer: Did you get an explanation at any point?

P1: In May a receptionist told me the referral had been rejected because a box on the form was not filled in. That was in February. So for three months it sat there rejected and nobody rang me. Nobody rang the GP either as far as I can tell. When I asked why nobody had told me she said the system does not do that. The system does not do that. I have thought about that sentence a lot.

Interviewer: What did you do then?

P1: I made the GP redo it and I stood in the surgery while she did it. I am not normally like that. I am the person who says thank you and leaves. But by then I had learned that if you are polite and you wait your turn you just wait forever. The people who get seen are the ones who push, and that is not fair on people who cannot push.

Interviewer: Did you eventually get an appointment?

P1: In August. Nineteen weeks after the second referral, so about eight months from the first one. The consultant was fine, he was thorough, he spent forty minutes with me and I have no complaint about him at all. He said it was probably manageable and if I had come in earlier it would have been simpler. That is the bit that sits badly. Not the waiting itself, but being told at the end that the waiting made it worse.

Interviewer: Is there anything that would have made a difference?

P1: A text message. Genuinely. If somebody had said, we have got your referral, you are number four hundred, it will be eighteen weeks, I would have got on with my life. It is not the wait that breaks you, it is not knowing. You cannot plan around a wait you cannot see.

Interviewer: How are things now?

P1: Physically better. But I do not trust it. When they said they would send me a follow-up in six months I wrote it in my diary and set two reminders, because I assume now that if I do not chase it, it will not happen. That is what the whole thing has taught me and I do not think that is a good lesson for a hospital to teach anyone.
