Interviewer: Take me back to when you were referred.

P3: It was after the second time in A&E. They said the GP would refer me urgently and urgent meant two weeks. I held on to those two words, urgent and two weeks, and I counted the days. When day fifteen came I panicked. I genuinely panicked, I was shaking on the phone.

Interviewer: What happened when you rang?

P3: The lady said there was no urgent flag on my referral, it had gone through as routine. She was kind, she was not being difficult, she just read me what was on her screen. But I remember standing in my kitchen thinking, if I had not rung today I would still be waiting for something that was never coming. And then the anxiety of that. What else is on a screen somewhere that is wrong about me?

Interviewer: How did you manage that period?

P3: Badly at first. I was not sleeping. I was googling at three in the morning which everyone tells you not to do and everyone does. My mum kept saying no news is good news and I wanted to scream because no news was exactly the problem.

Interviewer: Did anything change?

P3: Yes. I started keeping a folder. Every call, the date, the time, the name of the person, what they said. When I rang again I could say, on the fourth of April at ten past two I spoke to someone called Karen and she told me this. It changed the conversation completely. Suddenly I was not a woman being emotional on the phone, I was somebody with a record.

Interviewer: Why do you think that made a difference?

P3: Because they could not tell me it had not happened. And I think, honestly, because it made me sound like the kind of person who might complain properly. I hate that that works. I should not have to sound like a threat to get an appointment. But it worked, and I would tell anyone to do it.

Interviewer: When were you finally seen?

P3: Eleven weeks after the A&E visit. And the appointment was good, I want to say that. The consultant listened, she did not rush me, she apologised for the wait which nobody else had done. That apology mattered more than I expected it to. It was the first time anyone had acknowledged that the last three months had been happening to a person.

Interviewer: How do you feel about it now, looking back?

P3: Two things at once. I feel stronger, because I learned how to advocate for myself and I did not know I could do that. And I feel angry, because you should not have to become a different person to get healthcare. My cousin has no English and she would never have made those calls. She would still be waiting. She is still waiting, actually, that is not hypothetical.

Interviewer: What would you want the service to know?

P3: That the wait is not empty time. People assume you are just sitting there. You are not, you are working, you are parenting, you are trying to hold a job while you do not know if something serious is wrong with you. Tell us where we are in the queue and half the damage disappears.
